\chapter{Results and Discussion}
\label{chap:results}

\section{Experimental Setup}
All experiments ran on the simulator of Chapter~\ref{chap:meth} on an Intel Core i5-12450H laptop, using the scripts in the \texttt{scripts/} folder, from which every table can be regenerated (setup and seeds as in Section~\ref{chap:meth}; 3,524 runs, 1,797 simulated hours). Delay is the stopped delay, with 95\% confidence intervals and paired $t$-tests over ten seeds.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Signal Safety Audit}
Every step of every run was checked for conflicting greens, missing yellows, and yellow or all-red shorter than 3~s or 1~s. Across all 3,524 runs, covering all seven controllers and 1,830 preemption events in the main runs alone, \textbf{none of these violations occurred}. The audit also exposed a flaw in an earlier preemption version that resumed without a release interval, which led to the release states of Figure~\ref{fig:preemption}.

\subsection{Experiment 1: Comparison Below Capacity}
In Table~\ref{tab:below} the adaptive controller reduced mean stopped delay by 35.3--36.1\% relative to the 20~s fixed plan, in all ten seeds at every level ($p<0.001$), and the 95th-percentile delay fell by 37.1--41.2\%. However, the Webster plan, actuated control and max-pressure control achieved the same or slightly lower delay (37.5--39.7\%, 37.2--39.9\% and 37.3--39.8\% below the fixed plan; between $-$6.8\% and $-$2.9\% relative to adaptive). Below capacity, therefore, most of the gain comes from replacing an untuned 20~s plan with short, demand-responsive greens (mean adaptive green 9.5~s at medium demand). Three-level density control was worse than the fixed plan at medium and high demand (+29.0\% and +28.9\%), as its 20--30~s greens outlast the queue. The discharge hold changed delay by between $-$1.2\% and +2.5\% relative to the adaptive controller, and throughput changed by at most 0.9\%, since all levels are far below capacity.

The original adaptive rule lost 18.9\% of capacity relative to fixed timing ($p$ $<$ 0.001): with both directions saturated its greens fell to the 8~s minimum (mean 8.1~s), as explained in Section~\ref{sec:hold}. With the discharge hold the mean saturated green rose to 37.6~s and capacity to +9.8\%. Max-pressure suffered the same loss as the original rule ($-$18.8\%), since moving vehicles also leave its pressure count, whereas actuated control (+9.6\%) and the Webster plan (+11.6\%), which runs the maximum green above capacity, did not.

Demand was then swept from 0.6 to 1.5 times capacity with blocked arrivals kept in an entry queue (Table~\ref{tab:sweep}, Figure~\ref{fig:above}). The original rule beat fixed timing only at 0.6$\times$: at capacity its delay was 148.7~s against 12.2~s, its queue grew by 28.9 vehicles per minute, and queues reached the link start during 78\% of approach-time. Actuated control behaved like the hold (10.6~s at capacity and 52.8~s at 1.2$\times$, against 10.6~s and 48.7~s), and max-pressure degraded like the original rule. With preemption on, the same pattern held with smaller margins (capacity $-$8.7\% and +3.2\%), because preemption also cuts the hold's long greens.

\subsection{Experiment 3: Sixty-Minute Peak Period}
In a 60-minute experiment, demand rose from 0.6 to 1.2 times capacity (ramping over 10--15~min), stayed there for 15~min and fell back to 0.6 by 35~min (Table~\ref{tab:peak}, Figure~\ref{fig:peak}; preemption off, ten paired seeds; the Webster plan timed for the average demand). The queue, including vehicles waiting to enter, peaked at 495 vehicles under fixed timing, 255 with the hold, 254 with actuated control and 1214 with the original rule. After the peak, the queue returned to its pre-peak level in 13.9 min under fixed timing and 7.3 min with the hold, whereas the original rule did not recover within the hour and max-pressure 28.7 min (9 of 10 runs did not recover). Entries were blocked for 68, 36 and 172 approach-minutes (fixed, hold, original); the hold served 11394~veh/h during the peak against 10574 for fixed timing and 8244 for the original rule; and total delay changed by $-$57.3\% with the hold, $-$57.6\% with actuated control and +356.1\% with the original rule. Managing a congestion episode therefore depends above all on not shortening greens while queues are saturated.

\subsection{Experiment 4: Emergency Preemption}
Emergency vehicles (1\% of arrivals) triggered 12.4--23.4 preemptions per hour. At medium demand, preemption cut their mean stopped delay from 8.87~s to 0.28~s under fixed timing and from 3.65~s to 0.35~s under adaptive control ($p<0.001$), but other traffic paid +3.1\% under fixed timing, +9.3\% under adaptive control and +7.2\% with the hold, because each preemption discards the density-responsive timing. Emergency vehicles are far more frequent here than in normal traffic, so the real cost would be smaller.

\subsection{Experiment 5: Ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} changes one part at a time on the same seeds. Counting vehicles without weights or multipliers raised delay by 22.7\% and 26.6\% at medium and high demand; removing only the queued/moving distinction raised it by 35.1\% and 33.0\%, whereas setting every class weight to 1 changed it by $-$3.9\% and $-$3.2\%. The benefit of the score thus comes from distinguishing queued from moving vehicles, not from the class weights. Raising $\theta$ to 1.5 lowered delay slightly ($-$4.9\% and $-$4.1\%), and the hold, with any zone length, left delay unchanged below capacity.

\subsection{Experiment 6: Vision Pipeline}
On seeds 1--4 (9,600 frames, 14,874 vehicle instances) the generic cascade reached 4.1\% precision and 8.2\% recall (Table~\ref{tab:detector}). 98.6\% of the false positives recur at the same spot, i.e.\ the cascade fires on background texture rather than on vehicles; recall varies by class (car 7.1\%, motorcycle 11.8\%, auto 7.6\%, bus 7.2\%, truck 7.7\%, fire\_truck 3.7\%, ambulance 14.3\%) and distance (0-20 m 5.4\%, 20-40 m 16.9\%, 40-60 m 17.4\%) and is lowest near the stop line. Without upscaling or without histogram equalization the cascade found essentially nothing, and a 2$\times$ upscale raised precision to 63.3\% but cut recall to 3.7\%. Only 0.6\% of vehicle instances carried a confirmed track, and tracked speed had a mean absolute error of 0.53~m/s over 82 estimates against vehicles moving at only 0.56~m/s, since tracks are confirmed almost only on queued vehicles: a negative result, as the pipeline gives no usable kinematics for moving traffic. Dropping the low-confidence matching stage or the motion model changed little, because recall is the limit. This is why the evaluated controllers read vehicle states from the simulator; accuracy on real footage is unknown.

\subsection{Experiment 7: Validation of the Simulated Kinematics}
\label{sec:kin}
Queue discharge was measured as at a real junction: headways of queued vehicles crossing the stop line in saturated fixed-time runs (five seeds), averaged from the fifth vehicle on. The Highway Capacity Manual base is 1900 veh/h/lane (a 1.89~s headway) with about 2~s start-up lost time~\cite{ref24}. The model of the main results discharges cars every 0.51~s (7,102~veh/h/lane; Table~\ref{tab:kinematics}), because without a reaction time a follower may trail by only the 2~m standstill gap. With $\tau = 1.6$~s in Eq.~\eqref{eq:safetau} the headway is 1.92~s (1,877~veh/h/lane), within 1.2\% of the reference; start-up lost time ($-$0.8~s) stays below field values.

All comparisons were repeated with $\tau = 1.6$~s (Webster re-timed for 3600~veh/h). Below capacity, delay fell by 33.7--35.4\% for the adaptive controller and 31.9--39.2\%, 35.0--39.5\% and 34.7--38.9\% for Webster, actuated and max-pressure. Relative to the calibrated fixed-time capacity (6,148~veh/h), the original rule reached $-$14.6\% and Webster +6.3\%, but the hold with its 10~m zone only $-$3.7\% (mean saturated green 17.4~s): vehicles leaving a queue now spread to gaps of about 20~m, so a 10~m zone empties between them and the green gaps out too early. The zone is thus a kinematics-dependent parameter, as the passage time of a real actuated controller is set on site. Re-tuned on the tuning seed to 20.0~m, the hold reached +6.1\% and actuated control +6.1\%, against +6.3\% for Webster.

\subsection{Experiment 8: Imperfect Sensing}
To measure the cost of the exact-state assumption, the vehicle-reading controllers ran behind a sensor model that reports only what a camera would: vehicles within 60~m, refreshed at 2~Hz, without classes, each detected with a given recall, optionally with phantom detections at fixed spots like the cascade's static false positives, and finally the measured pipeline of Experiment 6; preemption reads true flags. Table~\ref{tab:sensing} gives delay at medium demand and 0.9 times capacity (medium / 0.9$\times$). Below capacity the controllers tolerated missed detections well, because missing vehicles only shortens greens, which costs little when queues are short. Near capacity, sensing mattered: the discharge-hold controller's delay rose from 7.8 s with exact states to 8.5 s with a perfect camera view and 14.4 s at 50\% recall, against 8.8 s for the fixed plan. Phantom detections were the most damaging error for the gap-out rule: a phantom inside the 10 m zone never moves, so the green never gaps out and runs to the maximum (medium-demand delay 11.9 s, +64.8\% relative to fixed timing). With the measured pipeline, every vehicle-reading controller was worse than the fixed plan near capacity (discharge hold 162.9 s against 8.8 s). At medium demand max-pressure and adaptive still beat the fixed plan, because phantoms appear on both directions and largely cancel in their comparisons, whereas actuated and adaptive + hold were held at long greens by phantoms in the stop-line zone (11.9 s against 7.2 s). Closing the loop with the present detector would harm traffic; a detector must at least suppress static false positives near the stop line, and its recall matters most near capacity.

\subsection{Experiment 9: Calibration of the Density Weights}
\label{sec:calib}
The hand-set weights were calibrated by random search over the class weights (0.25--4; emergency 1--10), the queued/moving ratio (1--12) and $\theta$ (1.0--1.6) of the hold controller, evaluating 14 candidates on tuning seeds 1001--1002 at medium demand, high demand and 0.9 times capacity (the car weight stays 1, since Eq.~\eqref{eq:green} compares ratios). The best candidate (motorcycle 1.89, auto-rickshaw 3.02, bus 1.67, truck 0.93, emergency 1.91, queued/moving ratio 2.94, $\theta$ = 1.37) was compared with the hand-set weights and a plain count on held-out seeds 1--10. On the held-out seeds the calibrated score changed delay by $-$2.0\% (medium, $p$ = 0.374), $-$4.9\% (high, $p$ = 0.036), +2.2\% (0.9x capacity, $p$ = 0.014) relative to the hand-set weights, whereas a plain vehicle count was worse by +23.2\%, +22.7\%, +13.4\% respectively. The plain count remained clearly worse, confirming the ablation. The parameters are fitted to this simulator and must be re-fitted on field data before deployment; the search is a reproducible procedure, not a learned controller.

\section{Discussion}
Two results qualify the usual claim that density-based control beats fixed timing. Below capacity, the gain over an untuned 20~s plan is not specific to density scoring: a Webster plan, actuated control and max-pressure control achieve the same. Near capacity, density-only switching is worse than fixed timing, because it ends greens while the served queue is still discharging. With the discharge hold, the controller matches the best strategies below capacity and comes close to the highest capacity measured (Webster's, +11.6\%), while its rule fits in one line, its red time is bounded and its safety is audited at every step. Its results are, however, almost identical to plain actuated control, which uses no density score: the gap-out test, not the score, does most of the work, and the score's remaining role is to decide when a cleared green hands over. Resuming after preemption with the higher-scoring direction is a direct improvement to test.

The research questions of Section~\ref{sec:contrib} are answered as follows: variable greens keep safe change intervals (no violation in 3,524 runs); preemption serves emergency vehicles without delay but costs other traffic more under adaptive control than under fixed timing; near and above capacity the original rule fails and the hold corrects it; and much of the gain below capacity comes from replacing an untuned plan, not from the density score.

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Simulation only:} no turning movements, lane changes, pedestrians or driver variability, and no calibration against field data, so absolute delays are not field predictions.
  \item \textbf{Kinematics:} the main model has no reaction time, so queues discharge too fast; the calibrated model matches the saturation headway but lacks start-up delay, so real short greens would cost more and the original rule's capacity loss is if anything understated. The hold's zone must suit local spacing (10~m for the default model, 20.0~m for the calibrated one).
  \item \textbf{Arrivals and baselines:} arrivals are deterministic headways at a redrawn rate, not Poisson, and emergency vehicles are far more frequent than in reality; the baselines are re-implementations with ideal sensing, and Webster knows the true demand.
  \item \textbf{Sensing and hardware:} control uses exact states and perfect emergency detection; the vision pipeline was tested only on synthetic frames and is too weak to drive control. A Raspberry Pi 4 deployment (roughly INR 6,000--8,000 in components, detection estimated 5--10 times slower than the laptop) is an estimate to be verified on actual hardware.
\end{itemize}
